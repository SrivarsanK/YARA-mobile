# **Product Requirements and Technical Architecture Document: Next-Generation Transit Tracking and Predictive Arrival System**

## **Executive Summary**

The modernization of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) demands solutions that transcend the limitations of legacy vehicle tracking platforms. A pervasive and critical flaw in contemporary public transit applications is their rigid adherence to discrete, trip-bound state architectures. Traditionally, transit systems track a physical vehicle only within the isolated context of its currently active scheduled trip. Consequently, when a vehicle is traversing an outbound route from Station A to Station B, it remains entirely invisible to commuters waiting for the subsequent inbound return trip from Station B back to Station A1. This technological blind spot yields a false indication of zero vehicle availability for inbound passengers, propagating severe wait-time anxiety, reducing public trust in the transit network, and ultimately causing a deterioration in ridership satisfaction1.  
The proposed transit tracking system fundamentally redesigns this operational paradigm by treating the transit vehicle as a continuous, perpetual asset across chained routes. By implementing a sophisticated two-way route tracking architecture, the system surfaces impending vehicle availability long before the vehicle reaches its turnaround terminus1. To support this advanced capability, the platform integrates an intricate compounding Estimated Time of Arrival (ETA) calculation engine capable of simultaneously predicting the remaining outbound travel time, the dynamic terminal halt duration (dwell time), and the subsequent inbound travel time to the waiting customer1.  
Because high-fidelity predictions rely entirely on uninterrupted spatial data, the platform is engineered to function flawlessly within severe low-bandwidth environments. It discards payload-heavy HTTP tracking protocols in favor of lightweight Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) pipelines4. To mitigate the inevitable degradation of Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) signals in urban canyons and tunnels, the hardware client relies on a multi-modal tracking approach, fusing raw GPS telemetry with a crowd cellular network fallback utilizing cell tower triangulation1. Furthermore, the system incorporates an autonomous, passive Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) packet sniffing module to generate real-time passenger density estimations without incurring the massive capital expenditure of vision-based hardware8. This comprehensive document outlines the exhaustive product requirements and technical architecture necessary to construct, deploy, and scale this highly resilient transit prediction ecosystem.

## **Core Problem Statement: The Trip-Bound Visibility Gap**

In existing public transit frameworks, data is structured linearly. A bus operating on Route X (Station A ![][image1] Station B) is treated by backend databases as a completely distinct entity from the bus that will soon operate on Route Y (Station B ![][image1] Station A), even if the exact same physical vehicle and driver are servicing both routes1. From a data modeling perspective, the vehicle essentially ceases to exist in the public-facing application at the moment it reaches Station B, only reappearing when it formally initiates the state parameters for the return trip1.  
This creates an operational failure known as the trip-bound visibility gap. A commuter standing at a stop midway along the return route (Station B ![][image1] Station A) queries the application for an incoming bus. Because the bus is currently three stops away from completing its outbound journey to Station B, the application's database filters it out of the query results. The commuter is informed that no vehicles are available, prompting them to abandon the public transit network in favor of a private rideshare alternative.

## **Two-Way Continuous Asset Tracking via GTFS Realtime**

To ensure that the transit tracking platform is globally interoperable and standard-compliant, the backend state machine must structure and publish its continuous asset tracking data strictly utilizing the General Transit Feed Specification (GTFS) and its real-time extension, GTFS-Realtime (GTFS-RT)11. Originally developed to standardize public transportation schedules, GTFS has evolved to accommodate complex trip chaining, which is the foundational mechanism required to solve the trip-bound visibility gap.

### **Static Data Architecture and Block Transfers**

The static GTFS dataset consists of a series of interconnected comma-separated values (CSV) files, primarily agency.txt, routes.txt, trips.txt, stops.txt, stop\_times.txt, and calendar.txt13. The essential component for enabling two-way continuous tracking resides within trips.txt through the implementation of the block\_id field2.  
In transit operations, a "block" refers to the sequential itinerary of trips operated by a single physical vehicle throughout its daily schedule14. By assigning the identical block\_id to both the Station A ![][image1] Station B trip and the subsequent Station B ![][image1] Station A trip, the transit routing engine recognizes that the trips are chained2. This programmatic linkage explicitly signals to the backend that a passenger could theoretically remain on the vehicle across the terminus—a concept known as an "in-seat transfer" or "block transfer"2.  
For the routing engine to validate and process a block transfer, several stringent prerequisites must be satisfied within the GTFS static files. The consecutive trips sharing the block\_id must operate on the same day or consecutive days (to account for midnight crossovers), must not possess overlapping active timeframes, and must belong to the identical route\_type2. Additionally, if the route is frequency-based (meaning vehicles arrive at regular intervals rather than adhering to a strict minute-by-minute timetable), the exact\_times field within the frequencies.txt file must be set to an integer value of 1, explicitly defining rigid departure constraints2.  
When the passenger queries the application for a ride on the inbound route, the backend database traverses the GTFS block\_id index. It identifies that the vehicle assigned to the requested inbound trip is currently active on the preceding outbound trip. Rather than hiding the vehicle, the user interface surfaces the vehicle on the map, rendering it with a distinct visual indicator (e.g., a desaturated icon or a badge stating "Completing prior route")1.

### **GTFS-Realtime Protobuf Publishing**

The real-time state of the network is published using GTFS-RT, which serializes data using Protocol Buffers (protobuf)—a language-neutral, platform-neutral binary exchange format that is exponentially faster to parse and more bandwidth-efficient than JSON or XML payloads11. The backend prediction engine acts as the GTFS-RT producer, generating a gtfs-realtime.proto feed that is fetched by client microservices every 10 to 30 seconds11.  
The GTFS-RT feed will concurrently publish three distinct entity types to maintain the continuous state machine11:

> 1. **VehiclePositions:** This entity transmits the immediate geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude), bearing, and velocity of the physical asset12. This allows the user interface to smoothly animate the vehicle's progress. Crucially, this entity contains the occupancy\_status enumerated field, which will be populated by the crowdsourced passenger density algorithms detailed later in this document12.  
> 2. **TripUpdates:** This entity transmits the dynamically calculated arrival and departure time predictions11. To support the compounding ETA requirement, the system will not only publish a TripUpdate containing a StopTimeUpdate array for the currently active outbound trip, but it will also proactively publish a TripUpdate for the subsequent inbound trip tied to the same block\_id. This allows the calculated ![][image4] variable to be injected directly into the delay metrics of the upcoming route, ensuring seamless visibility12.  
> 3. **Alerts:** This entity transmits textual descriptions of unforeseen network anomalies, mechanical failures, or severe detours, allowing the system to communicate qualitative context alongside quantitative predictions12.

## **Low-Bandwidth Telemetry: Overcoming HTTP Limitations**

The continuous asset tracking architecture is entirely dependent on the uninterrupted flow of telemetry from the physical vehicle to the cloud backend. Historically, IoT deployments and transit tracking applications have defaulted to utilizing Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) RESTful APIs for this data transmission4. However, HTTP was architected for document retrieval across reliable web infrastructures, making it highly ill-suited for the volatile, high-frequency, low-bandwidth environments characteristic of urban and rural cellular transit networks19.  
The proposed hardware telemetry client mandates the exclusive use of Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT). Originally engineered in the late 1990s for monitoring remote oil pipelines over highly constrained satellite links, MQTT is a data-centric, asynchronous publish-subscribe protocol that operates over persistent Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) connections5.

### **Comparative Performance Benchmarks**

The architectural shift from HTTP to MQTT yields massive dividends in bandwidth preservation, battery conservation, and latency reduction, all of which are critical for edge devices operating on degraded 2G or EDGE cellular fallback networks1.  
Unlike HTTP, which utilizes a synchronous request-response model requiring a client to initiate a request and await a response before proceeding, MQTT's publish-subscribe paradigm decouples the hardware client from the backend server4. The vehicle acts as a publisher, transmitting binary payloads to a central MQTT Broker (e.g., Eclipse Mosquitto or HiveMQ) mapped to specific topics, such as fleet/vehicle\_id/telemetry19.  
The structural overhead of these two protocols demonstrates a stark contrast. HTTP requests carry verbose, ASCII-formatted metadata headers that frequently exceed 800 bytes per transmission, regardless of how small the actual data payload is5. Conversely, MQTT features a highly compressed fixed header of merely 2 bytes5. When a transit vehicle is configured to transmit its GPS coordinates, speed, and heading every five seconds to maintain real-time UI animation, the cumulative bandwidth waste of HTTP becomes mathematically unsustainable on congested cellular networks1.

| Protocol Attribute | Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT) | Hypertext Transfer Protocol (HTTP) |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **Architectural Model** | Asynchronous Publish/Subscribe | Synchronous Request/Response |
| **Minimum Header Size** | 2 bytes (Fixed binary header) | 800+ bytes (Verbose ASCII text headers) |
| **Connection State** | Persistent, long-lived TCP connection | Re-established per cycle (unless Keep-Alive is configured) |

| **Bandwidth Efficiency** | Extremely High (Requires \~50x less traffic for bulk time-series data) | Low (Poor payload-to-overhead ratio) |

Extensive performance benchmarking demonstrates that MQTT consistently outperforms HTTP in mobile IoT contexts. In secure session tests utilizing Transport Layer Security (TLS), MQTT required approximately 10% less raw traffic than HTTP for singular packet exchanges22. However, when scaling up to bulk transmissions (e.g., transmitting 1,000 distinct time-series messages), MQTT over SSL completed the task in 5.9 seconds using 283 kilobytes of data, whereas HTTP utilizing keep-alive connections required 115 seconds and consumed over 15 megabytes of bandwidth22.

### **QoS Levels and State Management**

MQTT integrates three explicit Quality of Service (QoS) levels, allowing developers to precisely tune the balance between delivery guarantees and bandwidth consumption:

* **QoS 0 (At most once):** The message is transmitted without requiring an acknowledgment. This is ideal for high-frequency GPS pinging where a dropped packet is immediately rendered obsolete by the subsequent ping one second later4.  
* **QoS 1 (At least once):** The broker guarantees delivery but may transmit duplicates4.  
* **QoS 2 (Exactly once):** The highest level of reliability, utilizing a four-step handshake. This will be utilized for critical state changes, such as a vehicle explicitly declaring it has arrived at the terminal Station B4.

Furthermore, MQTT provides a critical feature known as Last Will and Testament (LWT)21. If a vehicle enters a subterranean tunnel and its persistent TCP connection is ungracefully severed, the MQTT broker instantly detects the timeout and automatically publishes a predefined LWT message to the backend4. This enables the backend routing engine to immediately transition the vehicle's icon into an "estimation mode," preventing the application UI from freezing and keeping the user fully informed of the degraded state.

## **Multi-Modal Location Tracking and Fallback Mechanisms**

The continuous prediction of compounding ETAs is highly sensitive to location data loss. While the Global Positioning System (GNSS) provides excellent accuracy under open skies, its reliance on direct line-of-sight communication with orbital satellites makes it intrinsically fragile in urban environments. Severe multipath fading, signal reflection off glass skyscrapers (urban canyons), and total signal occlusion in tunnels or transit hubs frequently result in catastrophic telemetry dropouts23. To maintain state integrity, the hardware module must implement a robust, multi-modal fallback architecture.

### **Cellular Tower Triangulation via Unwired Labs and OpenCelliD**

When the edge hardware detects a failure in the GNSS fix—defined by a total loss of satellite lock or a Dilution of Precision (DOP) metric exceeding acceptable thresholds—the system will immediately pivot to a cellular network fallback mechanism1. Modern cellular modems constantly monitor the surrounding radio frequency environment to maintain connection with the optimal cellular tower.  
The edge client will extract the network identifiers of the primary connected tower and adjacent visible towers via AT commands. These identifiers strictly include the Mobile Country Code (MCC), Mobile Network Code (MNC), Location Area Code (LAC), and Cell Identity (CID)25. This lightweight, four-parameter payload is transmitted over the MQTT pipeline to the backend.  
The backend prediction engine will utilize APIs such as Unwired Labs or large-scale open-source databases like OpenCelliD to resolve these identifiers into geographic coordinates6. OpenCelliD represents the largest global database of cell towers, providing precise location data that allows the backend to triangulate the vehicle's position without requiring direct satellite interaction27. While the spatial accuracy of cellular triangulation (often a radius of 100 to 500 meters) is significantly inferior to GNSS (often a radius of 3 to 10 meters), it guarantees that the vehicle is never entirely lost from the system6.  
To ensure the scalability and rigorous testing of this fallback mechanism across diverse network operators globally, QA engineers can utilize advanced frameworks that geographically decouple the physical Subscriber Identity Module (SIM) card from the cellular modem29. By tunneling the low-level SIM card protocol over an internet connection to remote-controlled modems situated in various international territories, the development team can validate the system's ability to seamlessly execute cell-tower handoffs and MQTT reconnections across hundreds of roaming environments without requiring physical field testers to travel29.

### **Sensor Fusion via Hybrid Kalman Filtering**

A significant UX problem arises when a system abruptly switches between high-accuracy GNSS data and low-accuracy cellular triangulation data. If the cellular triangulation API places the bus 300 meters away from its last known GNSS position, a naive user interface will visually "teleport" the bus across the map, instantly corrupting the ETA prediction calculations.  
To mitigate this, the backend spatial processing engine will implement an Extended or Hybrid Kalman Filter7. The Kalman Filter is an optimal estimation algorithm that operates continuously in a two-step recursive process: prediction and update7.

> 1. **Prediction Phase:** The filter calculates the vehicle's expected next state (latitude, longitude, and velocity) based on its known historical trajectory, its physical constraints, and wheel-based speed data extracted via the vehicle's CAN bus telemetry7.  
> 2. **Update Phase:** When a new measurement arrives via MQTT, the filter computes a weighted average of the predicted state and the newly observed measurement7.

The weighting factor, known as the Kalman Gain, is dynamically adjusted based on the mathematical certainty of the incoming data source7. High-resolution GNSS pings are assigned a high weight, forcing the algorithm to closely track the satellite data. Conversely, when a cellular triangulation coordinate arrives with a large uncertainty radius, the filter suppresses its weight. Instead of teleporting the vehicle to the exact triangulated point, the algorithm smooths the position along the known static geometric path of the transit route, constrained by the vehicle's previously known velocity7. This sensor fusion guarantees fluid visual animation and prevents catastrophic spikes in the ETA prediction models.

## **Machine Learning for Compounding ETA and Halt Time Prediction**

The architectural leap from a trip-bound system to a continuous tracking system shifts the burden of ETA calculation from simple arithmetic to highly complex multi-horizon time-series forecasting. The core variables—![][image3], ![][image4], and ![][image5]—are profoundly sensitive to non-stationary environmental dynamics, spatial topologies, and human behavioral patterns23.

### **The Evolution of Transit Prediction Models**

Early methodologies for transit prediction relied heavily on linear regression and simple historical averaging. As machine learning advanced, the industry pivoted to ensemble methods like eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Random Forest algorithms, which effectively learned nonlinear relationships between travel times and weather or time-of-day features3. However, these models struggle to internalize the sequential, time-dependent nature of transit data.  
To capture sequence dependencies, researchers adopted Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), specifically Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) models3. LSTMs mitigate the vanishing gradient problem, allowing the network to retain a memory of past traffic states to inform future predictions. While effective for short-term, single-leg predictions, LSTMs experience significant performance degradation when asked to execute multi-horizon forecasts spanning vast timeframes, such as predicting the inbound travel time of a bus that is currently 45 minutes away from finishing its outbound trip3.

### **Implementation of Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT)**

To calculate the compounding ETA formula accurately, the system's predictive core will be built upon a Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) architecture10. Introduced to overcome the limitations of recurrent networks in multi-horizon forecasting, the TFT leverages attention mechanisms to process entire temporal sequences in parallel, identifying complex long-range dependencies without losing resolution10.  
The TFT is explicitly engineered to handle the heterogeneous data types inherent in public transit routing:

> 1. **Static Covariates:** These are features that remain constant throughout the prediction window, such as the route's topological geometry, distance between stops, and the assigned GTFS block\_id10.  
> 2. **Known Future Inputs:** These are variables whose values are definitively known in advance, such as the day of the week, scheduled departure timetables, and meteorological forecasts (e.g., predicted snowfall)3.  
> 3. **Observed Past Inputs:** These are sequential data points leading up to the current moment, including the vehicle's historical velocities over the past hour, recent delay cascades, and passenger boarding density data37.

Through a mechanism known as the Variable Selection Network, the TFT dynamically assigns importance weights to each input feature at every specific time step35. For example, if a severe blizzard is occurring, the TFT will intelligently increase the weight of the meteorological data and decrease the weight of historical summer travel times. The self-attention heads allow the model to recognize both short-term anomalies (a sudden traffic accident delaying the ![][image3] leg) and long-term seasonal patterns (a historical propensity for massive delays at a specific intersection during Friday evening rush hour)10.

### **Dynamic Halt Time Prediction and Spatiotemporal Graph Modeling**

The most volatile variable in the continuous asset formula is ![][image4]. Assuming a static dwell time at the terminus (e.g., a rigid 15-minute driver layover) is a catastrophic error1. If the vehicle experiences a 10-minute delay on the outbound route, a human driver or dispatch system will likely abbreviate the terminal halt time to 5 minutes to restore schedule adherence for the inbound route. If the system fails to predict this dynamic human behavioral adjustment, the ETA for the waiting commuter will be wildly inaccurate.  
The TFT will be trained specifically to predict ![][image4] by analyzing the inverse correlation between accumulated route delay and scheduled rest periods, ensuring the compounding math remains precise37.  
To further enhance the spatial awareness of the predictions, the backend may integrate elements of Spatiotemporal Multi-Task Graph Transformers (SMT-GraphFormer)37. Public transit networks are inherently graph-structured; stations operate as nodes, and the asphalt roadways operate as edges37. By projecting the data onto a graph representation, the transformer can analyze spatial interdependencies. It can calculate how a delay at Node C on Route 1 will organically cascade and impact the inbound departure time at Node A on Route 2, recognizing the shared physical road corridor37. This multi-task architecture permits the simultaneous, interdependent prediction of boarding density, terminal dwell times, and arrival delays37.

## **Crowdsourced Passenger Density Estimation**

Providing accurate ETAs resolves the temporal anxiety of public transit. However, to fully optimize the passenger experience, the system must resolve spatial anxiety by providing real-time data on vehicle crowding40. A bus arriving precisely on time is useless to a commuter if the vehicle is filled beyond physical capacity. Access to density metrics empowers commuters to make intelligent decisions, naturally balancing network loads as users voluntarily opt to wait for a subsequent, emptier vehicle40.  
Historically, transit authorities have relied on high-resource, infrastructure-heavy solutions to calculate Origin-Destination (OD) matrices and occupancy levels. These include Automated Passenger Counters (APCs) mounted over doorways, Automated Fare Collection (AFC) systems via RFID smart cards, or advanced computer vision systems utilizing YOLO object detection algorithms over CCTV feeds32. While precise under ideal conditions, vision-based systems suffer severe performance degradation during extreme overcrowding, drastic lighting shifts, or unique deployment stressors common in sub-Saharan African transit networks or heavy snow environments24. Furthermore, high capital costs and privacy concerns frequently prohibit their deployment across entire fleets32.

### **Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) Packet Sniffing**

The proposed hardware client will bypass these limitations by deploying a low-cost, participatory sensing methodology: passive Wi-Fi and Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE) packet sniffing8.  
The fundamental premise of this approach relies on the ubiquitous nature of modern smart devices. Smartphones, tablets, and wearables constantly execute discovery protocols to find nearby networks or pairable devices. In the 802.11 (Wi-Fi) standard, this takes the form of "active scanning," where the mobile device periodically broadcasts unencrypted Probe Request frames into the surrounding environment8. Similarly, Bluetooth devices periodically transmit BLE beacons42.  
By equipping the transit vehicle's MQTT edge client with a standard Wi-Fi/BLE radio interface configured to operate in promiscuous or monitor mode, the system can passively capture these radio frequency transmissions8. The hardware extracts the unique Media Access Control (MAC) address of the broadcasting device and measures the Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI) of the packet8. Because the vast majority of urban populations carry at least one smart device, the aggregate count of unique MAC addresses serves as a highly correlated mathematical proxy for the physical human passenger count8.

### **Algorithmic Mitigation of Overestimation and Underestimation**

A naive summation of captured MAC addresses will yield severely distorted density metrics. The system must algorithmically resolve two contradictory statistical errors: overestimation and underestimation8.  
**The Overestimation Problem:** The radio interface will inevitably capture probe requests from devices that are entirely unaffiliated with the transit vehicle. This includes pedestrians walking on adjacent sidewalks, drivers in parallel traffic, and individuals sitting inside nearby roadside buildings8. To filter out this environmental noise, the hardware client will execute a rigid spatial filtering algorithm utilizing two distinct metrics:

> 1. **RSSI Thresholding:** Radio waves attenuate as they pass through physical barriers. A smartphone located inside the metal chassis of the bus will exhibit a significantly higher RSSI compared to a device located thirty meters away behind a brick wall8. The system will establish a baseline RSSI cutoff, discarding any packets that fall below the threshold.  
> 2. **Temporal Persistence:** A pedestrian passed on the street will remain within the sensor's detection radius for only a few seconds. Conversely, a true passenger will exhibit temporal persistence, broadcasting probe requests continuously over an extended duration8. The filtering algorithm will enforce a rule mandating that a unique MAC address must be observed across multiple scanning intervals (e.g., maintaining presence for \> 120 seconds) before it is formally classified as an onboard passenger.

Advanced deployments can pipe the RSSI, temporal persistence, and scanning frequency data into lightweight supervised machine learning classifiers deployed directly on the edge hardware. These models can achieve a high degree of binary classification accuracy, accurately separating "inside bus" signals from "outside bus" signals9. Field experiments utilizing BLE sensors demonstrate that these systems can estimate passenger counts with a mean absolute error of just 2.49 passengers42.  
**The Underestimation Problem:** Not every physical passenger carries a smart device, and among those who do, a subset will have their Wi-Fi and Bluetooth interfaces manually deactivated to conserve battery8. Therefore, the filtered MAC count will always represent a fraction of the true occupancy. To correct this, the backend engine will apply a statistical correction function derived from localized ground-truth calibration studies9. If empirical testing on a specific route reveals that 65% of physical passengers emit trackable radio signals, the raw filtered MAC count is scaled mathematically to generate the final, highly accurate density metric displayed to the end user.

### **MAC Randomization and Privacy Preservation**

The passive collection of MAC addresses poses a significant privacy and security risk. A MAC address is a globally unique hardware identifier; tracking it in plaintext over time could allow malicious actors to reconstruct an individual's daily transit behaviors42. Furthermore, modern operating systems (iOS and Android) have implemented MAC randomization, where devices broadcast a fake MAC address during active scanning specifically to thwart unauthorized tracking.  
To navigate these challenges, the edge hardware client must enforce strict anonymization protocols at the source. Before any data is transmitted over the MQTT pipeline to the cloud, the captured MAC addresses must be cryptographically hashed (e.g., using a SHA-256 algorithm combined with a daily rotating cryptographic salt).  
This one-way hashing guarantees that the backend system can accurately tally the number of unique identifiers present on the bus during a specific trip to determine the density metric, but it is mathematically impossible to reverse-engineer the hash to identify the specific user, nor can the user be tracked across subsequent days once the salt rotates8. Regarding MAC randomization, empirical studies demonstrate that randomized MACs are typically rotated at intervals longer than a standard bus commute, meaning temporal persistence tracking within a single trip remains viable44.

## **Hardware Client and Edge Processing Specifications**

The hardware footprint required to execute this vast array of tasks—GNSS tracking, cellular network scanning, Wi-Fi/BLE probe sniffing, and MQTT telemetry publishing—must be designed for extreme cost-efficiency and power conservation.  
The onboard module will consist of an off-the-shelf microcontroller architecture (such as an ESP32 or a hardened Raspberry Pi variant) interfaced with a multi-constellation GNSS module (supporting GPS, GLONASS, and Galileo) and an LTE Category M1 (LTE-M) or Narrowband IoT (NB-IoT) cellular modem21. These cellular modems are specifically designed for low-bandwidth, low-power IoT applications and natively support the AT commands required to pull Cell ID parameters for triangulation25.  
A critical architectural decision is the division of computational labor between the edge hardware and the cloud backend. To minimize cellular data consumption, the edge client will perform the heavy filtering of the Wi-Fi/BLE probe requests locally. Rather than streaming thousands of raw, noisy MAC addresses and RSSI readings to the cloud every second, the microcontroller will execute the RSSI thresholding, temporal persistence logic, and cryptographic hashing locally. It will subsequently publish a highly compressed, pre-processed aggregate metric to the MQTT broker, ensuring the system remains strictly within the low-bandwidth operational constraints1.

## **Cloud Architecture and API Interfaces**

The cloud backend serves as the orchestration layer, fusing the raw telemetry, executing the machine learning models, and serving the data to the client applications.

> 1. **Ingestion Layer:** A clustered, highly available MQTT Broker (e.g., HiveMQ) will handle thousands of concurrent, persistent TCP connections from the fleet19.  
> 2. **Stream Processing Layer:** Tools like Apache Kafka or Apache Flink will consume the MQTT topics in real-time. This layer executes the Hybrid Kalman Filter, smoothly fusing the precise GNSS coordinates with the coarse cellular triangulations7.  
> 3. **Prediction Layer:** The pre-processed spatial and occupancy data is piped into the Temporal Fusion Transformer (TFT) model10. The TFT pulls additional contextual data (weather APIs, traffic APIs, static GTFS files) to continuously recalculate ![][image6] and ![][image4]3.  
> 4. **Distribution Layer:** The system generates the updated gtfs-realtime.proto binary feed11. To serve the proprietary mobile application efficiently, a series of microservices will sit between the mobile app and the GTFS-RT feed. Because the GTFS-RT feed encompasses the entire city's transit network17, it is too large for a mobile client to download over a cellular connection. The mobile app will send an HTTP or lightweight WebSocket request containing its geographic bounding box and route query. The microservice will parse the GTFS-RT protobuf in memory, filter it down to the specific chained trips requested, and return a minuscule JSON payload directly to the user17.

## **Conclusions and Implementation Directives**

The architectural synthesis articulated in this document provides a comprehensive blueprint for solving the most persistent friction points in intelligent transit tracking. By entirely abandoning the outdated trip-bound data paradigm, and structurally chaining trips via the GTFS block\_id specification, the platform guarantees continuous asset visibility across operational boundaries1. Commuters waiting on a return leg will no longer be misled by false indications of zero availability.  
To execute this vision reliably, the development team must adhere strictly to the deployment of MQTT for edge telemetry. The data efficiency, payload compression, and battery preservation offered by MQTT are non-negotiable requirements for operating on unreliable, low-bandwidth urban cellular networks4. This network resilience must be backed by a multi-modal hardware configuration, relying on GNSS where available, but seamlessly falling back to cellular tower triangulation smoothed by a Hybrid Kalman Filter to guarantee uninterrupted spatial awareness6.  
The transition to Temporal Fusion Transformers (TFT) represents the critical evolutionary step necessary for accurate compounding ETA calculations. Simple mathematical arithmetic cannot account for the behavioral elasticity of terminal halt times or cascading spatial delays; only an attention-based time-series model can evaluate the massive heterogeneous data sets required to deliver true multi-horizon foresight1. Finally, the integration of passive Wi-Fi and BLE crowdsourced density estimations equips the end-user with actionable occupancy data, democratizing network load balancing without violating passenger privacy or requiring prohibitive capital investments8. By integrating these sophisticated methodologies, the resulting transit tracking platform will deliver an unprecedented echelon of operational reliability, user visibility, and public trust.
